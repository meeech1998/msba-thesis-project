When a crisis call comes in to Missoula 911, a cascade of decisions gets made very fast: whether to route the call to the Mobile Support Team (MST), to MPD or MCSO, or to both agencies as a co-response, and whether that encounter ends in a voluntary handoff, an involuntary hold, use of force, or injury. Missoula began documenting those decisions in March 2024 through the Mental Health Referral Form (MHRF), creating one of the most granular encounter-level datasets of its kind.

This study uses nearly two years of that data, 861 encounters involving 609 unique individuals, to examine the internal mechanics of how crisis encounters unfold and what drives their outcomes.

Executive Summary

Encounter severity is predictable. Repeat contact is not.

This study analyzed 861 crisis encounters documented through Missoula's Mental Health Referral Form between March 2024 and February 2026, representing 609 unique individuals. The analysis set out to answer four questions: what behavioral and situational features predict whether an encounter becomes severe, whether encounter-level information can predict who returns to the crisis system, how dispatch routes calls across response models, and whether encounters group into distinct typologies. The results show that severity is predictable from features observable at the scene (test AUC = 0.719), that repeat contact is not (Cox test C-index = 0.461), and that the cluster with the lowest severity rate generates the largest share of repeat encounters.

Data 861 MHRF encounters across 23 months, covering 609 unique individuals, with structured fields for behavioral indicators, call type, responding agency, commitment criteria, force, and injury outcomes.
Methods L2-regularized logistic regression and PCA for severity, Cox proportional hazards and Kaplan-Meier for time to return, K-modes clustering on 10 presentation features, and chi-square tests for response model triage.
Deliverables A four-component severity index, a working severity prediction model, four encounter typologies, and five evidence-based recommendations for the Missoula crisis response system.

Research Questions
The Four Questions This Study Answers
This analysis examines the internal mechanics of how crisis encounters unfold and what drives their outcomes, rather than measuring aggregate program effects.
01

Severity Prediction

What behavioral and situational features predict whether a crisis encounter will be severe, defined as involving involuntary intervention with commitment criteria met, use of force, subject injury, or injury to others?

02

Repeat Encounters

Can encounter-level information predict whether an individual will return to the crisis system, and if so, how quickly?

03

Dispatch Routing

How does the dispatch system route calls across response models, and does this routing reflect a systematic severity gradient?

04

Encounter Typology

Do crisis encounters group into distinct typologies, and if so, what do those typologies reveal about the relationship between severity and repeat contact?

Methodology
Analytical Pipeline
All analyses were conducted in Python using NumPy, with statistical methods implemented from first principles to ensure transparency and reproducibility. The pipeline combines descriptive analysis, two predictive models, survival analysis, and unsupervised clustering, each targeting a specific research question.
1

Data Preparation

861 encounters from the MHRF. Two binary outcome variables were constructed: a severity flag combining four escalation pathways (involuntary handoff with at least one commitment criterion met, force used, subject injury, injury to others), and a person-level repeat contact flag for individuals with more than one encounter in the dataset.

Feature Engineering
2

Descriptive Analysis

Temporal trends, behavioral indicators, housing status, call type, geographic distribution, and response model variation were examined to characterize the dataset and identify whether cross-sectional comparisons were confounded by changes over time.

Chi-Square Tests
3

Severity Model

L2-regularized logistic regression (lambda = 0.001) on 17 predictors: eight behavioral indicators, substance involvement, housing status, probable cause, three call type indicators, and age. Responding agency was deliberately excluded as a predictor. Evaluated on an 80/20 train-test split (n=688 train, n=173 test) with 500-iteration bootstrap standard errors. PCA applied as a robustness check: 11 components captured 82.7% of variance, yielding a test AUC of 0.719.

Logistic Regression + PCA
4

Repeat Contact Analysis

Cox proportional hazards model on 609 unique individuals, modeling time between first and next encounter with right censoring for individuals who had no repeat contact by the end of follow-up. Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by housing status and first-encounter severity, with log-rank tests for between-group differences. Performance evaluated by concordance index (C-index).

Cox PH + Kaplan-Meier
5

Encounter Typology

K-modes clustering on 10 presentation features (eight behavioral indicators, substance involvement, housing status) with k = 4 and 30 random initializations, selecting the partition with the lowest total dissimilarity cost. Severity and repeat contact were excluded from the clustering inputs and examined only as outcomes.

Key Findings
What the Data Revealed
Three central findings emerged from the analysis, each grounded in a specific model and each pointing toward specific policy changes for the Missoula crisis response system.
Finding 1

Dispatch Triage Is Working

Severity rates range from 4.1% (MST-only) to 50.3% (co-response) to 65.0% (MPD-only), confirmed by chi-square (χ² = 198.1, p < 0.001). The gradient persists within call types (e.g., suicidal calls: MST 1.4%, co-response 42.4%, LE 53.8%), meaning dispatchers route based on cues beyond call category. This is a signal that triage is effective.

Finding 2

What Predicts Severity

The four strongest predictors: Confusion (+0.70), Disorganized Speech (+0.67), Suicidal/Self-Harm Call (+0.66), and Substance Involvement (+0.62). On the protective side: welfare checks (−1.05), scared/frightened (−0.47), and housed status (−0.46). Depression, the most common indicator, is not a significant predictor.

PCA robustness check: 11 components captured 82.7% of variance, achieving test AUC of 0.719. Severity is predictable from features observable at the scene.

Finding 3

Repeat Contact Is Not Predictable from the Encounter

The Cox proportional hazards model test concordance dropped to C-index = 0.461, worse than random. Encounter-level features cannot predict who returns. Only substance involvement reached significance (protective, HR = 0.60). What makes an encounter severe is not what brings someone back.

Kaplan-Meier curves by housing: unhoused individuals return faster (log-rank p < 0.01), with 33% returning by day 400 vs. 18% of housed. First-encounter severity did not predict return (p = 0.11). Chronic crisis utilization appears driven by structural factors between encounters, not encounter-level features.

Policy Implications
Recommendations for Missoula
Five evidence-based recommendations, each tied directly to findings from the analysis.
Recommendation 01

Structured Follow-Up for Low-Acuity Encounters

The Minimal Presentation cluster generates 45% of all repeat contacts despite having the lowest severity rate (33%). A structured follow-up protocol for encounters without clear clinical markers could reduce the single largest source of repeat system contact.

Recommendation 02

Recognize Dispatch Triage as an Asset

The severity gradient across response models is real and persists within call types. MST's 1.4% severity rate among suicidal calls reflects effective routing, not easier calls. This system should be preserved and studied as a model for other communities.

Recommendation 03

Expand Behavioral Health Response Capacity

MST currently operates 10 AM–8 PM, seven days a week. Any call outside that window is handled by law enforcement alone, regardless of risk level. Extending MST into evening and overnight hours would let more low-risk calls be resolved through behavioral health and free officer capacity for calls that genuinely require it.

Recommendation 04

Target Training to the Strongest Severity Predictors

The severity model points at four specific presentations: confusion, disorganized speech, suicidal/self-harm calls, and substance involvement. CIT Academy content focused on recognizing and de-escalating those signals (rather than general mental-health awareness) would land closer to the encounters most likely to escalate.

Recommendation 05

Separate Repeat-Contact Interventions from Encounter-Level Work

Encounter-level features cannot predict who will return (Cox test C-index = 0.461). Housing is the one factor with a clear link to return speed. Reducing chronic crisis utilization means investing in housing stability, outpatient linkage, and case management between encounters, not in optimizing the encounter itself.

Limitations
What This Study Cannot Say
Transparent boundaries on the claims this analysis can and cannot support, drawn directly from the written report.

Dispatch Selection

Responding agency is not randomly assigned. Within-call-type analysis confirms the severity gradient reflects triage, but a fully causal interpretation would require an instrumental variable design.

Reporting Bias

The MHRF is completed by the responding professional, introducing variation in documentation. MST and MPD staff may document differently based on training and institutional norms.

Model Generalizability

The severity model (test AUC 0.719) identifies associated features but should not be used as a clinical screening tool without further validation. The repeat model (C-index 0.461) confirms encounter-level features have no predictive value for return.

Study Window

The 14-month window may not capture long-term trends. The dataset lacks diagnosis, treatment history, and post-encounter outcomes. Individual timestamps are unavailable, limiting analysis of after-hours staffing effects.

Data Collection

Paper-based forms with manual data entry introduce inconsistency and an average 18-day delay. Person IDs were reconstructed from partial information; some linking errors may remain.

Hi, I'm Michelle. I'm finishing my MSBA at the University of Montana, and this is the project I've been living inside for the last year.

I started it because I wanted to know what happens after the 911 call. Missoula's crisis system sees hundreds of people a year, and almost none of what it learns about them gets fed back into how the system actually runs. That gap bothered me enough to spend a year in a spreadsheet.

Somewhere around encounter number 400, I stopped being able to look at a row without thinking about the person in it. That's the part of analytics work I care about most: keeping the numbers honest without forgetting they're about someone's worst day. This portfolio is my attempt to hold both.
